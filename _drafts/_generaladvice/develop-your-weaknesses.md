First identify how you identify yourself. Now identify your greatest weaknesses in that space. That's what you should develop.

There is always another language, tool, or technique you could learn. So how do you decide which one deserves your time? I start by asking what kind of work I want to be able to do. That's what I mean by identifying how you identify yourself. It can be the work you do now or the work you're trying to grow into.

Take "software engineer." It's a broad label. If what you want is to own a feature from the interface through to the database, that gives the label some meaning. You can ask which parts of that work you can handle today and which parts regularly stop you. If every database change requires someone else to take over, I'd pay attention to that gap. Getting comfortable with a straightforward database change could expand the work you can take responsibility for.

Now imagine you prefer frontend work and your team has people who own the database. You have exactly the same gap, but it matters less to the work you want to do. You may get more out of learning accessibility or improving how you test the interface. That's why I'd identify the kind of work first. A weakness only matters in relation to something you're trying to accomplish.

I don't think the label itself has much power. You can call yourself a full-stack engineer and still mean something quite different from the next person using that label. What matters is that the identity points you toward work you care about. [Daphna Oyserman's writing on identity-based motivation](https://dornsife.usc.edu/daphna-oyserman/wp-content/uploads/sites/232/2023/11/oyserman2015ibm.pdf) describes how the identity that feels relevant in a situation can affect what we choose to do and how we interpret difficulty. That supports the value of asking who you're trying to become. It doesn't give you a ranked list of skills to learn.

For that list, I'd look at actual work. Where do you keep needing help? What kinds of tasks do you avoid? What do people you work with notice? A gap that repeatedly interrupts something you want to do deserves more attention than a topic that happens to embarrass you. And needing help once probably tells you very little. I'd look for a pattern before declaring something my greatest weakness.

Even then, the gap needs a more precise name before you can develop it. "I'm bad at debugging" is hard to act on. "When I investigate a failure, I change several things at once and lose track of what worked" gives you a habit to practice. On the next bug, reproduce the failure, change one thing, and check what happened before changing anything else. Ask someone to review how you reached your conclusion, not just whether the final fix works.

There is a research connection here. In [their paper on deliberate practice](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i225s14/files/2014/04/Ericsson-1993-article.pdf), K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe, and Clemens Tesch-Römer describe practice aimed at specific weaknesses, with feedback on performance. They studied musicians and drew on work in other fields. That supports practicing a defined skill and checking whether you improve. Applying the idea to debugging is my example; the paper doesn't tell us that every person's weakest skill should always come first.

In fact, there are times when I'd choose a strength instead. If you already do the core parts of your job well, getting much better at something valuable may benefit you and your team more than bringing your least-used skill up a little. You may be the person who can make a confusing interface understandable or explain a complicated system to others. There's a cost to spending all your development time fixing weaknesses while leaving that strength where it is.

Research gives us a reason to take this possibility seriously. In [two experiments with university students](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/enhancing-psychological-capital-and-personal-growth-initiative-wo/), Maria Christina Meyers and colleagues compared development focused on strengths with development focused on deficiencies. In the second experiment, both groups became more active about their development over three months, with a larger increase in the strengths group. They measured initiative toward development, not mastery of a particular job skill. I wouldn't use that study to declare strengths the winner in every situation. I would use it to question any rule that always sends us to our weaknesses.

There's another point where this advice stops helping: when the weakness is already good enough. Database work might remain your weakest area even after you can make the changes your role calls for. At that point, more practice might bring a small benefit while another skill opens up new work. "Greatest weakness" is a moving target, and the goal is to become capable of doing the work, not to make every skill equally strong.

I'd also revisit the identity now and then. You may have called yourself a full-stack engineer for years while discovering that you care most about frontend work. If the label keeps sending you toward skills you no longer want to use, change the label. And if you don't yet know what kind of work you want, trying a few things is probably more useful than drawing up a list of deficiencies.

Finally, check whether the problem belongs to the individual at all. If everyone on a team struggles because priorities change each week, that is unlikely to be solved by each person working on time management. You can only choose a useful development goal once you understand what's getting in the way.

I come back to three questions: What work do I want to do? Which gap repeatedly limits that work? What would "good enough" look like? If I can answer those, I have a reason to practice and a point at which to decide what comes next.
